Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD) Machine

This project is a thermal evaporation physical vapor deposition (pvd) setup. It was inspired by a few youtube videos by Advanced Tinkering, Huygens Optics, and Thought Emporium. Indeed, PVD is used in many industries to apply a thin coating; it is used to manufacture everything from mirrors to computer chips to drill bits. This project took several months. I bought working components, repaired broken machines, and designed and manufactured many parts out of steel, plastic, glass, aluminum, and copper. In the end, I consider this project a tremendous success. I was able to reliably achieve vacuums of down to 0.2pa and coat glass, metals, and plastic. The next step would be finding ways of making the coating adhere better to the substrate as the coatings the present system produces can be relatively easily removed.

System Overview

Physical vapor deposition works by vaporizing (boiling) a solid target source in a high vacuum, sending metal atoms flying along a line-of-sight trajectory to condense onto a target. The overall system can be split into several subsystems:

  • High Vacuum Chamber:Aluminum and glass vacuum vessel with CNC bolt-on KF25 and KF50 flanges down to 0.2pa
  • Two Stage Pumping and Pressure Measurement: Rotary Vane roughing pump in conjunction with a Turbomolecular pump. Pressure measurement using a hybrid pirhani and hot-cathode pressure gauge.
  • Thermal Deposition: Adjustable voltage and hundreds of amps heat up and boil most metals.
  • Liquid Cooling: Closed-loop water cooling high current electrical contacts and a turbomolecular pump

Build & Testing Gallery

Components Breakdown

I think the best way to go over my experience building this system is by looking at each of the components.

  1. Edwards E2M5 Rotary Vane Roughing Pump

    I picked up this vacuum pump for about $280 on Ebay. While I saved quite a bit of money compared to the $1500-$2000 cost of a brand-new or rebuilt one, it was in a very sorry state indeed. The pump was filled with rusted bolts, congealed vacuum oil, completely dried seals, and paper-insulated wiring with bare copper showing.

    I completely disassembled the pump, scrubbed the castings with a steel scrubber, replaced the rusted bolts and o-rings, and cut custom gaskets. Ultimately, after spending around $150 on McMaster-Carr parts to restore this pump, I got it working reasonably well. The only function that I was unable to restore was the gas ballast mechanism as the long and thin copper tube was entirely clogged. After refilling with black gold vacuum pump oil, the pump worked very well for me.

  2. Custom Oil Mist Filter

    Running the pump in my basement, I quickly realized that things near the pump were steadily getting coated in a thin layer of oil. The pump from ebay had actually come with an oil mist filter of its own, but the filter element was completely blocked and a replacement was $200. As this wasn't on the high vacuum side, I thought it would be okay to make myself. It's a simple 3d print; the filter ensuring that the trajectory of any oil splatters would result in the oil draining back into the pump, and a compartment for activated alumina beads to filter the remaining oil mist.

    Printables Link

  3. FL20K Foreline Trap

    I originally didn't think this was necessary, but when I let the air back in after the first time I pumped down the chamber, I saw and smelled a cloud of smoke instantaneously appear as oil vapors condensed. I was able to pick component up on ebay for only $40

  4. Edwards EXT255H Turbomolecular Vacuum Pump

    The roughing pump can get down to <10pa, which is arguably enough if you have an absolutely leak-proof system and don't care that much about the quality of the coating. This turbomolecular pump should only be turned on 100pa and achieves a higher vacuum by spinning at 20,000rpm and using the blades to literally knock out individual air molecules.

    The vacuum chamber container I used isn't big enough to fit the turbomolecular pump directly. I used a ISO100 to KF50 flange adapter cone to connect the pump.

    A professionally rebuilt unit sell for about $6000. I got one on ebay for $600. Luckily for me, the pump was in excellent condition and the manual was easily available online. Simply connect 80v and connect a switch between the start pin and ground.

    Beware, though, when browsing ebay. If you buy the 24v version of the EXT255H and EXDC80 drive module instead of the 80v version, you will likely have a very hard time finding documentation. The pinouts for the 24v version are given in the manual for the Edwards Turbo Instrument Controller.

  5. Water Cooling Reservoir

  6. 24V DC Power Supply

    24v for the Vacuum gauge, an 80v step-up buck converter for the turbomecular pump, and a 12v step-down converter for the water cooling fans and pump

  7. Inficon BPG400 Bayard-Alpert Pirhani Vacuum Gauge

    This is a very nice vacuum gauge. It uses a Pirhani gauge in combination with a hot-cathode gauge. At higher pressures, it uses the Pirhani gauge. As the pressure drops and the Pirhani gauge becomes ineffective, it automatically turns on the hot cathode B-A gauge which glows a brilliant orange. The gauge has some great features, including the ability to simply supply 24v and get an analog signal out. I just used a voltmeter and a lookup table to read out the pressure.

  8. Glass Vacuum Chamber

    This is quite likely the most dangerous part of the whole system. A massive 2gal thick glass jar as the vacuum chamber. Should it implode, it would have released approx. 1atm*2gal=770J of energy, about 2 9mm bullets.

    The jar itself is from Walmart. It's the "Anchor Hocking Heritage Hill Glass Jar with Lid 2 Gallon". To seal with a gasket, the lip of the jar has to be flat, which it was not: there was text and a lip on the jar. To fix this, I picked up a carbide grinder bit for my dremel. I then wet-grinded the surface flat. However, it still wasn't flat enough to seal against a gasket, so I CNC'd an aluminum flange (literally just a ring) and bonded it to the glass jar with Loctite EA-1C.

  9. Variable Transformer

    To vary the amount of power put into evaporation, this variac adjusts the voltage sent across the evaporation boat.

  10. Custom High Current Transformer

    It's a rewound microwave oven transformer. I replaced the secondary with two turns of 00 wire. From 120v, the output is about 2-2.5v.

  11. Water Cooling Pump

  12. Water Cooling Radiator

  13. Custom Bolt-on KF25 Flange

    I'm very proud of this. I looked far and wide for a component like this, but ultimately couldn't find one. The typical vacuum chamber has KF or ISO flanges welded on. Since I don't have that skill or equipment, I had these made which bolt on and seal with a face seal and an o-ring. This way, the chamber could easily be constructed by bolting a few of these to a flat baseplate. I made KF50 and KF25 versions. I used vented screws on the high vacuum side to prevent virtual leaks.

  14. Custom Water-cooled Contact and High-current Pass-through

    I'm very proud of this as well. The high current pass-throughs were made from a rod of copper, an aluminum KF25 weld flange, a 3d-printed insulation sleeve, and EA-1C vacuum safe epoxy. The water-cooled contacts are cnc copper. In the vacuum, there is a spring-loaded copper plate to maintain contact with the evaporation boat. Outside, I used a grounding clamp to electrically connect the 00 wire to the copper rod.

  15. Molybdenum Evaporation Boat and Sample

    I got these off ebay as well. Why are they Molybdenum and not Tungsten? Because they were a lot cheaper. To hold the target above the sample, I used some stainless steel armature wire.

  16. Viton Pipe Gasket

    From Mcmaster-Carr, this is the face seal for the entire chamber. Once the vacuum is somewhat established, the pressure sucks the jar down to compress and make a good seal. Before that, I used some Molykote high-vacuum grease to stick the gasket to the baseplate.

  17. Viton Tubing

    The first iteration of this setup had no water-cooled contacts. I had thought that it would be running for a short enough time that heat wouldn't be an issue. However, while it indeed wasn't an issue, I also never dared run it too long, lest the pass-through melt and the turbomolecular pump explode. Thus, I had to retrofit water-cooled contacts. There aren't many plastics suitable for high-vacuum, and Viton is one of the few.

  18. Water-cooling Pass-through

    As mentioned, the water-cooling was a retrofit. So the pass-through was very rudimentary. I drilled two holes in the baseplate, stuck two barb fittings through, and covered any gaps in EA-1C epoxy. It was basic, but it worked.

  19. 0.25" 6061 AL Baseplate

    This is the second iteration of the baseplate. In the first one, I hilariously didn't consider that the KF25 flanges needed clamps and I put the flanges too closely together.
